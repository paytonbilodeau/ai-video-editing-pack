# What a Good Edit Should Preserve

This guide explains the reasoning. Measurable defaults for pauses, breaths,
word-edge handles, take selection, non-speech sounds, pacing, punch-in scales,
captions, graphic timing, shorts and the QC checklist live in
[EDITING CRAFT NUMBERS](EDITING%20CRAFT%20NUMBERS.md).

The useful result is a video someone can follow and enjoy. A low duration or
a high number of cuts does not establish that. Choose the audience and tone
before deciding how tight the edit should feel.

## Make two cutting passes

First choose complete thoughts and the best takes. Remove agreed mistakes,
unnecessary repetitions, and dead time. A transcript helps locate them, but
can hide a stutter or attach words to the wrong time. Check the recording.

Then inspect the assembled edit closely. Play the final words before each join
with the first words after it. Trim unnecessary delay without losing a consonant,
a word ending, a useful breath, or a pause that gives the point weight.

Look inside retained sentences too. A long-silence pass can leave repeated
fragments, little hesitations, and tiny clips containing only a mouse click.
Decide what each fragment contributes before removing it. Do not delete every
short clip or every breath just because a script can find them.

For a tutorial, keep the action, its result, and enough time to read that result.
A still screen may contain important information; it may also be dead time.
The viewer's task decides. Review the complete sentence after tightening it.

## Vary the pace for a reason

A direct statement can move quickly. An unfamiliar idea needs more space. A
personal admission may work better without another cut. A reaction, joke, or
result can need a short hold. Preserve the creator's natural delivery unless
they ask for a different treatment.

Use another angle, a screen crop, or a graphic when it helps the viewer see or
understand something. Do not zoom on every sentence or add movement merely
because nothing has moved recently. A crop should preserve useful context and
enough resolution to remain clear.

## Direct visuals in ordinary language

A useful request names the thing, its action, the timing, its place, and its
purpose. For example:

```text
At the words "three separate files," show three simple file cards beside me.
Bring them in one at a time as I name them. Hold them still while I explain the
difference. Keep them clear of my face and captions. Use my approved style
reference, then remove them when I return to the next point. Keep it editable.
```

Choose a short representative section before building a long animation. Check
entrance, settled state, reading time, exit, sound, and the transition back to
the video. When the graphic has finished explaining, remove it or introduce the
next useful relationship. Permanent motion does not repair an unnecessary hold.

For graphics behind a person, create a separate background/graphic layer and
a masked foreground subject. Check hair, hands, motion blur, and occlusion
through movement. A successful still frame does not prove a clean moving mask.
Use a simpler side-by-side layout when masking damages the result.

## Add your own finishing touches

Ask for the exact project, timeline name, and saved version to review. Duplicate
that timeline before your changes. Keep the assistant's handoff so changes can
be compared later.

If it suits you, review at 1.5x or 2x to find repetition and long holds. Return to
normal speed for cut boundaries, audio, readability, motion, and final approval.
Fast playback can hide defects and does not establish the normal viewing rhythm.

Learn three actions in your editor: split at the playhead, ripple trim the start
to the playhead, and ripple trim the end to the playhead. A ripple trim closes
the resulting gap. Confirm track selection and linked audio first. Shortcuts
vary by app and custom keyboard preset; verify the action in Keyboard
Customization rather than guessing a key. Test on a duplicate timeline.

## Turn one correction into a useful instruction

```text
Compare your handoff timeline with my revised timeline. List the source ranges
I changed and show the before/after difference. Ask if the reason is unclear.
Separate a one-video choice from a preference I want you to reuse. Suggest a
short update to the relevant skill or my private editing profile, and keep the
previous version. Test the accepted rule on the next suitable passage before
claiming it helped. Do not undo my approved changes.
```

Useful lesson: "Preserve the result screen until the viewer can read the changed
value." Weak lesson: "Make everything tighter." Save a small example and the
condition under which the rule applies. A different recording may need a
different decision. These are saved instructions, not changes to model weights.

Timeline comparison depends on the editor connection. Use a supported API or
an exported XML/EDL when it preserves the relevant changes. An EDL may omit
effects, titles, and other details, so name what could actually be compared.
If neither route is available, ask the person for change notes and timestamps,
or compare the before/after exports. Record that as partial evidence: a final
video does not reveal every edit decision, and an export comparison does not
justify claiming the saved project was inspected.
